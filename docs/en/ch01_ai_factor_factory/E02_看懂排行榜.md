## 2. Four Metrics: Intuition and Pitfalls

### IC (Information Coefficient)

The cross-sectional correlation between factor values and future returns, computed daily.

- Intuition: **how accurate the directional prediction is**. IC=0.03 is already a level worth taking seriously in A-shares.
- Pitfall: the average IC can be propped up by a few extreme trading days — 49 of 50 days near 0 and one day at 0.3 averages to 0.006, yet also hands you a beautiful maximum.

### Rank IC

Uses rank correlation (Spearman) instead of Pearson.

- Why quants prefer it: robust to outliers. A stock up 20% on the limit and one up 10% are both "first place" to a rank.
- Practical habit: watch Rank IC's sign-consistency rate (share of positive days) — it exposes problems earlier than the mean does.

### ICIR

The mean of IC divided by the standard deviation of IC — **the stability of the prediction**.

- Intuition: a factor with mean IC 0.05 and ICIR 0.5 is better than one with mean IC 0.08 and ICIR 0.2.
- Pitfall: ICIR says nothing about turnover. A factor that fully turns over its book every day can have a great ICIR, but fees eat everything.

### Excess return

Portfolio return relative to a benchmark. The most intuitive — and the most pitfalls:

- **Who is the benchmark?** The same factor with a different benchmark can flip the sign of the excess. This campaign's futures leg used a 23-instrument equal-weight index; the A-share campaign once used CSI 300 — later it turned out the benchmark excluded limit-up stocks, so a substantial part of the "excess" was an artifact (detailed in Lecture 8).
- **Were costs included?** The factory backtest's default convention may not include real fees.
- **How is long-short done?** The qlib 0.9.6 backtester blocks shorting at three layers — the factory's long-short numbers actually came from a vectorized side-computation, yet another convention switch.

## 3. Factor "Combos" and "Versions": Why One Factor Has a Dozen-Plus Records

On the leaderboard, `momentum_20d` has 23 evaluations and 33 code versions. Three layers of structure:

- **Factor name**: one hypothesis ("20-day momentum")
- **Combos**: records of this hypothesis being repeatedly implemented and evaluated across different time windows/loops. 23 evaluations means the loop touched it in 23 different contexts
- **Versions**: code iteration history — the AI fixed bugs, tuned parameters, changed implementations

Here is a concept the leaderboard never states outright but this course uses constantly: **multiple testing**.

> The loop touched it 23 times, and what you see is the **best of the 23**. Flip a coin 23 times and one streak of heads is no surprise; test a factor 23 times and one good-looking excess is equally no surprise. 290 factors, each evaluated several times on average — this leaderboard is a **multiple-testing generator**.

The Lecture 1 discussion question can now be answered: run the loop 1000 rounds and the leaderboard's #1 becomes **less** credible — the larger the selection base, the more likely the top name is the luckiest noise. This is not an AI problem; it happens to human researchers manually trying 1000 factors too. The AI just accelerated it by orders of magnitude.

## 4. Three Things Self-Reported Results "Don't Tell You"

1. **Who is the benchmark** — excess is a relative quantity, and every benchmark convention choice (exclusion rules, weighting, tradability assumptions) seeps into the excess. The A-share campaign's lesson: benchmark excludes limit-ups → the long side naturally avoids limit-up stocks → a chunk of the "excess" is an artifact.
2. **Were costs included** — leaderboard numbers are mostly gross or semi-net. High-turnover factors (the momentum family with 33 versions is the hardest hit) frequently flip negative after net costs.
3. **How many times was it tested** — as in the previous section. For a good-looking top name, first check its combos/versions: the more evaluations a factor has, the higher the ceiling on the luck component of its best record.

A controlled-experiment preview: the leaderboard top of these 290 factors was later re-tested in the independent validation pipeline under pre-registered rules — **not a single one survived** (Lecture 5). Not that the factors were all worthless, but the correlation between "leaderboard rank" and "true validity" is far weaker than intuition suggests.

## 5. Lecture Summary

- IC for direction, Rank IC for outlier robustness, ICIR for stability — none of the three contains cost information
- The combos/versions columns are direct evidence of "how many times it was tested"; read these two columns first when reading a leaderboard
- The leaderboard is a multiple-testing generator: top of 290 factors self-reports +26.5%, all fail independent validation
- Three must-ask questions: who is the benchmark, were costs included, how many times was it tested

**Discussion question**: If you could add just one column to the leaderboard to keep yourself from being fooled, which column? (This course's choice: the pre-registered verdict — think it through before checking the answer)
