# Lecture 12 · The Discipline of Opportunity Screening: Before Diving In

> Chapter 5 · Distilling the Method (first lecture)
> Learning objectives: master the ex-ante screening framework for research directions — orthogonal discrimination, exclusion rules, opportunity-cost accounting; understand the "research ledger" as organizational memory.
> Prerequisite: the whole course (this chapter gathers the methodology of multiple campaign threads).

## 1. The problem: the bottleneck of research capacity is not ideas

After running a complete "factory + validation + shadow" system, you quickly discover the true constraint structure:

- **Ideas are not scarce**: ten "can this data make a factor" thoughts pop up every day
- **What is scarce is validation capacity**: every OOS window is a one-shot consumable; every piece of engineering investment (data procurement, panel construction, pipeline development) is real cost
- **The most expensive thing is a wrong direction**: three weeks sunk into a wrong direction loses not three weeks, but the correct directions that could not be validated during those three weeks

So the form of the question is not "what research to find" but "**by what rules to exclude research at the entrance**". This lecture's content comes from the opportunity-screening institution gradually solidified in real campaigns.

## 2. The first gate: the exclusion list (DO NOT RESEARCH)

More important than "what is worth researching" is a written-down "what we no longer research". The real exclusion list (excerpted; behind every entry is a real cost):

- **Falsified hypothesis families**: the z-fade spread-reversion family (all three sub-types show no reversion), the earnings-forecast event window (OOS direction reversed), the H1 dispersion Gate (opposite direction) — **the same hypothesis family in a different disguise is not a new hypothesis**
- **Data unobtainable**: the futures-options direction (source blocked, DATA_BLOCKED) — confirm the data first, then talk design
- **Already covered by existing assets**: directions whose return sources overlap heavily with validated strategies (the orthogonality requirement, see next section)
- **Exposure-timing types** (marked DO NOT RESEARCH YET): not never-do, but under the current evidence structure (style beta explains nearly all excess) there is no testable hypothesis — wait first, don't spin idle

The everyday value of this list is **defensive**: three months later you yourself will have forgotten why a direction was abandoned, and you will invest in it again — the exclusion list is written for your future self. The "YET" suffix design also matters: it distinguishes "falsified" (evidence against) from "shelved" (currently untestable); the two carry different implications for resource discipline, and mixing them would permanently bury revivable directions.

## 3. The second gate: orthogonal discrimination — four questions, each a veto

A new opportunity must pass four questions; failing any one knocks it out (a real institution, all four with veto power):

**① Is the money it earns already being earned by existing strategies? (return orthogonality)**
A new opportunity must show its return source is not shared with deployed/validated strategies. funding carry got greenlit because it answered this first question cleanly: funding cash flow, entirely distinct from price prediction.

**② Is the data it depends on something others can also easily obtain? (an honest assessment of data moats)**
If the answer is "everyone has it", then excess can only come from processing quality — which requires a concrete answer to "is my processing really better than others'", not "I feel we're more careful".

**③ Can its hypothesis be falsified? (testability)**
The entrance version of Lecture 3's "a mechanism that can explain any result is no mechanism": before entering research, write down "what result would make me admit it's wrong". If you can't write it, you don't enter.

**④ Can you afford the cost of validating it? (power budget)**
The entrance version of Lecture 7's "power retraction": roughly compute statistical power from expected effect magnitude and available data volume — a direction with insufficient power (say, testing a 3%-magnitude effect over a 350-day window) is formalism no matter how beautiful the design. **Not every good idea is worth doing now.**

Together, the four questions are four faces of one question: **can this opportunity's "truth" be known at an affordable cost?**

## 4. The third gate: the economic investment threshold

Opportunities passing the first two gates must also pass an **economic accounting**: data cost + engineering cost + validation-window consumption, set against "the expected economic value if validation passes" (a prior of magnitude × capacity × survival probability). This is not precise calculation but order-of-magnitude ranking — the purpose is to stop "technically interesting, economically meaningless" projects from crowding out capacity (both research organizations and researchers are naturally drawn to "interesting"; this gate forces "worth it" back in).

## 5. The research ledger: archiving the screening process itself

The rulings of all the gates above — passed, excluded, shelved — are recorded together with their reasons in the **research ledger** (a persistent document organized by campaign). Its three functions:

1. **Prevent recurrence**: exclusion rules only hold back your future self if written down (Section 2, above)
2. **Locking in re-review trigger conditions**: shelved entries carry "what change triggers re-evaluation" (e.g. "re-evaluate line D when options data becomes available in 2026-12"), giving "waiting" an exit rather than oblivion
3. **Booking negative results**: the complete record of every REJECTED/INCONCLUSIVE (hypothesis, verdict, numbers, disposition) is an asset — it prevents the same OOS window from being retested in disguise (the enforcement vehicle of Lecture 6's hypothesis-family embargo), and serves as prior evidence for similar future hypotheses

The underlying logic of ledger discipline carries on Lecture 3: **do not tamper with history; make history recomputable**. A research organization's memory that exists only in individual heads is no memory at all.

## 6. A complete screening example: how three surviving lines came to be

The direction review after the second round of the futures campaign (the six-direction matrix) ran these gates for real, and the result structure clearly shows how the gates work:

| Direction | Gate ruling | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| A cross-sectional factors | **Greenlit** | Return source orthogonal to existing lines; additional methodological value as the control baseline for the LLM factory |
| B time-series CTA | **Greenlit** | Different return source from cross-sectional; time-series convention already in place |
| C spread reversion (z-fade family) | **Excluded** | Inherits the prior REJECTED — the first gate intercepts it outright |
| D options | **Shelved (DATA_BLOCKED)** | Data unobtainable; re-review trigger condition registered |
| E event type | **Excluded** | Overlaps a falsified family |
| F basis / term structure | **Partially shelved (PARTIAL)** | Effect magnitude in doubt after D-9; watch line |

Note C and E: they were not "reviewed out" — they were **instantly killed** by the list, which is exactly the point of the first gate: turning the most expensive mistake (redoing what was falsified) into the cheapest ruling.

## 7. Lecture summary

- The true bottleneck of research capacity is validation resources and direction choice; the essence of a screening institution is **protecting expensive investment with cheap vetoes**
- Three gates: exclusion list (with the YET mechanism) → four orthogonality questions (veto power) → economic investment threshold
- The common core of the four questions: "can this opportunity's 'truth' be known at an affordable cost"
- The research ledger: rulings archived, re-review conditions locked in, negative results booked — the institutionalization of organizational memory
- The six-direction matrix example: falsified families killed instantly at the first gate is the highest cost-effectiveness moment of the whole system

**Reflection question**: someone proposes researching "AI sentiment indicators for A-share market timing". Run it through the four orthogonality questions: which question is most likely to stop it? Which is hardest to answer?
