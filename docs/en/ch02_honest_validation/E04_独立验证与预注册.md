# Lecture 4 · Why Independent Validation Is Needed: Pre-Registration and the Validation Pipeline

> Chapter 2 · Honest Validation (Lecture 1)
> Learning objectives: understand the institutional difference between "factory self-reported results" and "independent validation"; master core concepts such as pre-registration, DSR, and the one-shot out-of-sample verdict.
> Prerequisite: Chapter 1 (especially Lecture 2's "three must-ask questions").

## 1. Starting from One Number

Chapter 1 ended with a number: 290 factors, top self-reported excess +26.5%, zero survivors under independent validation. This lecture answers: **what exactly is "independent validation" independent of, and why are its conclusions more credible than the factory's numbers?**

First, a comparison. The factory backtest and independent validation do similar things — both compute factor returns on historical data. The difference is not in compute or code quality, but in **institutions**:

| Dimension | Factory built-in backtest | Independent validation pipeline |
|---|---|---|
| Data | Campaign panel (the same copy) | Independently pulled, independently cleaned recomputation panel |
| Rules | Can be adjusted while watching results (the loop is naturally so) | **Pre-registered**: decision rules written down and archived first |
| Verdict | A leaderboard ranking, no threshold | **One-shot verdict**: threshold pre-registered; once triggered, it is final |
| Target | Every factor gets a number | Only the very few candidates passing initial screening |
| Nature of conclusion | Descriptive ("how much did it return") | Adjudicative ("reject/inconclusive/support") |

In one sentence: the factory backtest answers "**what would happen if we used it**"; independent validation answers "**is it actually real**". The former is description; the latter is a statistical verdict.

## 2. Multiple Testing: Why "Adjust While Watching" Necessarily Produces False Positives

This is the first principle of all validation science, worth spelling out slowly.

Suppose all 290 factors are pure noise (zero correlation with future returns). Testing each factor at the 5% significance level, you expect 290 × 0.05 ≈ **14.5 "significantly effective"** factors. These 14.5 look **exactly like** real factors on the leaderboard — significance does not write "I am luck" on your face.

This problem already existed in manual human research (academia calls it a breeding ground for p-hacking); the LLM loop scaled up the base by two orders of magnitude and dropped the cost to zero — so multiple testing went from "a bias to watch out for" to "the system's dominant failure mode".

**The only institutional countermeasure is pre-registration**:

1. **Before looking at the data**, write down and archive: the candidate factor list, the validation data window, the decision thresholds, the statistical conventions, and "what to do if inconclusive"
2. At validation time, execute strictly per the archive; **post-hoc adjustment of any rule is forbidden**
3. A rule change = a change of experiment identity = the whole thing must be rerun, and the old conclusion is void

The essence of pre-registration is not ceremony but locking down the **researcher degrees of freedom before the results come out**. The "parameter-tunable overlay factor" you met in Lecture 2 consumes degrees of freedom inside the factor; "deciding which benchmark, which window, which threshold after seeing results" consumes degrees of freedom at the validation stage — the latter is more hidden and more lethal.

## 3. The Four Components of the Validation Pipeline

Using the real pipeline that accompanies this course (AIResearchPlatform, separate storage from the factory, independent data pulls):

### Component one: independent data recomputation

The validation panel is not copied from the campaign panel but independently pulled and recomputed from the data source. The reason comes straight from practice: the double-adjustment bug behind the carry factor's +18% hid in the campaign panel's construction code — if validation had directly consumed that panel, the bug would have been inherited as-is and the correction would never have happened. (The dedicated chapter on convention issues is Chapter 3.)

### Component two: pre-registered thresholds

Example thresholds used in the real campaign (written into the pre-registration document before any OOS data could be touched):

- **DSR (Deflated Sharpe Ratio)**: a multiple-testing correction on the Sharpe — you tried N strategies, so the significance threshold must rise accordingly. The larger N, the higher the bar, and the harder for the top name to clear it
- **Primary/secondary referees**: besides the primary criterion, a secondary criterion with a different convention (e.g., price convention vs funding-rate convention); when the two disagree, "supported" may not be declared
- **Per-year constraint**: a structure of "all returns come from one year" is disallowed — a single year's contribution above the cap is vetoed, preventing one market episode from being mistaken for a law
- **Orthogonalization opponents**: a candidate must retain residual alpha after orthogonalizing against known style factors (momentum, value, low-vol, liquidity); whatever is explained by existing factors does not count as a new discovery

### Component three: the one-shot out-of-sample verdict (OOS one-shot)

Out-of-sample data is a **consumable**: each use contaminates it once. The rule is that one OOS window permits exactly one verdict —

- SUPPORTED: advance to the next stage (shadow monitoring, Chapter 4)
- REJECTED: archive the conclusion; that hypothesis family goes on the "no re-test" list (preventing another shot at the OOS in a different costume)
- INCONCLUSIVE (e.g., insufficient power): freeze; you may only wait for new forward data or a new pre-registration; **lowering the threshold and retrying is forbidden**

### Component four: paper trail and reproducibility

Validation reports are archived on disk (data fingerprints, code versions, threshold values), recomputable by anyone at any time. This is not just scientific norms but also insurance for your future self — Lecture 9 will show that several major errata (including the +18% fake carry) were discovered and corrected precisely because "historical conclusions can be recomputed".

## 4. Three Real Verdicts from One-Shot Decisions

The three outcomes this institution produces each have real cases, forming the next two lectures:

| Verdict | Case | Numbers | Lecture |
|---|---|---|---|
| REJECTED | Earnings forecast event window | OOS spread **−0.49%** (direction reversed), discovery period also ineffective | Lecture 6 |
| INCONCLUSIVE | Index rebalancing flow | OOS **+1.00%**, p=0.062 — insufficient power, frozen | Lecture 6 |
| SUPPORTED | crypto funding carry | OOS **+2.37%/yr**, p<0.0001, passed all eight sub-checks | Chapter 4 |

Note the relative magnitudes of the three numbers: **the only surviving strategy annualizes 2.37%**; the two rejected ones had far more dazzling discovery-period numbers. Real alpha is small and ugly; fake alpha is big and plausible — this is not a coincidence but a structural feature of this industry, and every later lecture returns to it.

## 5. Lecture Summary

- The factory backtest is description; independent validation is verdict; the difference is institutional, not computational
- Multiple testing is the loop-mining's dominant failure mode; pre-registration is the only institutional antidote
- Four pipeline components: independent data, pre-registered thresholds (DSR/primary-secondary referees/per-year/orthogonalization opponents), one-shot OOS verdict, reproducible paper trail
- Each verdict has a real case: REJECTED (−0.49%), INCONCLUSIVE (+1.00%, p=0.062), SUPPORTED (+2.37%/yr)
