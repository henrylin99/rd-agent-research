    Lecture 8 · Benchmark Artifacts: When the Quantity "Excess" Itself Is Wrong¶

    Chapter 3 · Hidden Mines in Data Conventions (Lecture 2) Learning objectives: understand how benchmark conventions systematically manufacture fake excess; master how dividend-handling errors contaminate total returns; build the habit of "auditing down to the quantity-definition layer". Prerequisite: Lecture 5 (where the benchmark artifact is first mentioned).

    1. Case Overview¶

    During the A-share campaign's independent validation it was found that in the benchmark construction used by the leaderboard convention, limit-up stocks were excluded. This seemingly harmless detail systematically contaminated the quantity "excess return" itself. Recomputed with the honest convention:

    The biweekly-rebalanced top200 portfolio's "excess" over the benchmark vanished entirely. A substantial part of the earlier excess was an artifact created by the benchmark's definition.

    Lecture 5 called this the bottommost blade of the four-layer audit stack — however rigorous factor validation is, if the benchmark is wrong, the foundation of every conclusion is wrong. This lecture unfolds that blade in full.

    2. Mechanism: How Excluding Limit-Ups Becomes "Excess"¶

    Take the magic trick apart step by step:

    Step one: the benchmark contains no limit-up stocks. The benchmark portfolio excluded stocks that hit the daily limit that day (possibly a "tradability" consideration — you can't buy at the limit).

    Step two: the strategy portfolio does contain them. The factor's stock-selection logic neither knows nor cares whom the benchmark excluded — strong-momentum stocks and stocks that hit the limit get selected all the same.

    Step three: A-share limit-ups have next-day drift. Stocks that hit the limit (especially non-one-board ones) have positive next-day drift on average — a market-microstructure fact (sealing capital, sentiment continuation, the queuing effect under the price-limit system).

    Step four: the artifact sets. The strategy portfolio holds a basket containing "next-day positive drift", and the benchmark removed that component — the strategy did nothing right; it merely stood on the opposite side of the benchmark's exclusion rule. The more its picks lean toward limit-ups, the better the "excess" looks. Momentum-type factors (leaderboard heavyweights — recall the 33-version momentum_20d from Lecture 2) are naturally advantaged against a limit-up-excluding benchmark — a sizable chunk of their "alpha" was a gift from that benchmark rule.

    Note the toxicity of this artifact: it cannot be arbitraged. Trading this strategy live, you equally cannot buy the limit-up stocks (or you buy at the next day's gap-up open); the backtest "excess" corresponds to fills unavailable in reality. And it is yet another failure mode than Lecture 7's bug — the data isn't wrong, the code isn't wrong, the statistics aren't wrong; the definition of the quantity is guaranteed to mislead.

    3. The Hidden Degrees of Freedom in Benchmark Design¶

    This case exposes an often-ignored truth: the benchmark is a research decision with extremely high degrees of freedom, yet it is rarely treated as one. The same history, the same strategy — the benchmark options include:

    • Whole-market equal weight / exclude limit-ups / exclude ST / exclude suspended
    • CSI 300 / CSI 500 / CSI 1000
    • Market-cap weighted / equal weighted

    Every option is legitimate and has its reasons, and the combinatorial space is large enough to "tune" significant excess out of any mediocre strategy. Researchers rarely cheat deliberately — the more common path: try a few benchmarks and unconsciously remember the one with good-looking results. This is exactly Lecture 4's "researcher degrees of freedom" recurring at the quantity-definition layer — more hidden than parameter-layer freedom, because it hides under the name of "convention" and never enters the robustness-check checklist.

    The institutional countermeasure is isomorphic to factor validation: benchmark conventions go into the pre-registration. The campaign's actual disposition was creating excess_honest (honest-convention excess) as the sole convention for all subsequent reporting, and recomputing every number under the old convention. The naming itself is discipline — making "honest" an explicit attribute rather than a default assumption.

    4. Another Mine on the Same Layer: Dividend Double Deduction¶

    The A-share panel's second convention flaw (found in the same period): when constructing the return series, dividends on ex-dividend dates were deducted twice — total returns systematically understated.

    The correction formula is clean:

    True total return = (1 + panel return) / (f_ratio)²

    (f_ratio is the adjustment factor; dividing by f_ratio² exactly cancels the double deduction.) After correction, it aligned with independent tushare data with zero error.

    This case's teaching value lies in its conclusion structure:

    • Relative-quantity conclusions survive: relative rankings among factors, the signs of long-short spreads are largely unchanged under overall understatement — because the bias acts nearly proportionally on both sides
    • Absolute-quantity conclusions are all distorted: any "annualized X%" style statement of absolute level is void

    Generalized into an audit principle: how much a data bias damages your conclusions depends on whether they rest on differences or levels. Conclusions resting on differences (rankings, spreads, relative strength) are robust to same-direction bias; conclusions resting on levels (returns, volatility, the denominator of Sharpe) are damaged together. Check which kind your key conclusions depend on, and you know how much audit budget to spend on data conventions.

    5. Completing the Audit Stack: The Fifth Layer¶

    The Lecture 5 four-layer audit stack can now be completed with the final layer beneath the foundation:

    第 5 层  量的定义     基准构成 / 超额的定义 / 可比性     —— 这个量在度量你以为的东西吗
第 4 层  统计充分性   DSR / 多重检验校正
第 3 层  收益来源     正交化 / 归因分解
第 2 层  可实现性     换手 × 成本敏感性
第 1 层  数据地基     复权 / 日期戳 / 拼接               —— 第七讲、第九讲

    The fifth layer is the least audited, because when it breaks it throws no error: code runs normally, numbers come out normally, charts look nice. The limit-up-excluding benchmark ran the entire campaign before being exposed in one cross-reconciliation; the dividend double deduction was caught only by a strong check like "zero-error alignment against an independent data source".

    Two general countermeasures (proven effective in the campaign):

    1. Independent-source alignment check: align panel returns point-by-point against at least one independent data source; the error should be exactly zero — any non-zero points to a convention difference worth chasing
    2. Convention inventory: list every benchmark construction rule, adjustment method, and exclusion logic as an explicit checklist in the pre-registration document. A convention you cannot write down ("just the default one") is the biggest risk exposure

    6. Lecture Summary¶

    • Benchmark excludes limit-ups + strategy holds limit-up stocks + limit-up next-day drift = "excess" requiring no alpha whatsoever; and that excess is unobtainable live (you can't buy limit-up stocks)
    • The benchmark is a high-degree-of-freedom research decision and must enter pre-registration; the "honest convention" should be explicitly named and become the sole reporting convention
    • Dividend double deduction: relative-quantity conclusions survive, absolute-quantity conclusions are distorted — allocate audit budget by whether conclusions depend on differences or levels
    • The audit stack's fifth layer (quantity definition) fails silently; catch it with independent-source alignment and convention inventories

(Hint: the long-short "benchmark" is the pool itself; the long-only benchmark is an external index — through which path must the artifact enter returns?)
