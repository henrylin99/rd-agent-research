    Lecture 7 · The D-9 Erratum: How an Adjustment Bug Manufactured a Fake 18% Annualized Strategy¶

    Chapter 3 · Hidden Mines in Data Conventions (Lecture 1) Learning objectives: understand the adjustment mechanics of futures dominant-continuous contracts and how the "double adjustment" bug arises; master the concept of "plausibility bias"; understand the institutional handling of errata. Prerequisites: Lecture 2 (where the carry factor first appears), Lecture 4 (the pre-registration institution).

    1. Case Overview¶

    The futures round-two campaign's research matrix had an L2 line: a term-structure carry strategy (long the deeply discounted instruments, short the ones at premium, earning the spread convergence). The factory's discovery-period backtest gave:

    Discovery period annualized +18%, the most dazzling number of the entire campaign, beautiful t-value, reasonable per-year structure.

    Nine days later (the erratum event numbered D-9), an audit found a double adjustment in the panel construction. Recomputed after the fix:

    Discovery period +3.18%, t = 0.66, statistically indistinguishable from zero.

    A number fell from "nearly deployable" to "noise" with only one convention audit in between. The number itself deserves nine days of discussion, but more deserving is: why it nearly fooled everyone — including a validation institution that was already quite strict.

    2. Technical Background: Dominant-Continuous Contracts and Adjustment¶

    Futures have no single continuous ticker like stocks. When building a panel, one usually takes the "dominant continuous": each monthly contract expires and switches to the new dominant, and the segments are spliced into one continuous series. At the splice points (roll days) the two contracts have different prices (premium/discount), and splicing directly produces fake gaps — hence adjustment: at roll days, shift or ratio-adjust the historical series as a whole to eliminate the splice gap.

    This campaign's panel used settlement-price-splice adjustment: splicing anchored on settlement prices.

    The double adjustment bug's mechanism: two places in the construction pipeline each independently adjusted for the roll gap (one an explicit adjustment step, one hidden inside the return series construction). The two adjustments stacked, with the effect of over-correcting slightly in the same direction at every roll. 23 instruments × four years × several rolls per year — the error was not random noise but a systematic one-directional drift.

    This is the key: random noise cancels out and makes t-values ugly; systematic bias accumulates in one direction and makes t-values beautiful. The carry strategy naturally holds both long and short legs with holding periods spanning roll days — it swallowed the one-directional drift wholesale into its returns.

    3. Why It Nearly Fooled Everyone: Plausibility Bias¶

    This is the concept this lecture most wants to leave you with. Why wasn't the +18% suspected immediately?

    Because it looked too reasonable. Term-structure carry has literature precedent (commodity carry is a classic risk premium), and "18% annualized" lands within the expectation band of "this strategy is supposed to make money, and quite a lot of it". The psychological default when humans check numbers is: audit the outrageous, wave through the reasonable — and a high-quality bug produces exactly reasonable numbers.

    Contrast with a thought experiment: had the same pipeline error made the carry backtest show annualized −25%, it would not have survived the afternoon — too outrageous, someone would surely investigate. A bug in the wrong direction exposes itself; a bug in the right direction hides itself.

    Plausibility bias: the consistency of a number with prior expectations gets misused as evidence of the number's correctness. The two are independent — the bug doesn't understand finance; it just happens to lean the way you hoped.

    Corollary: for a result that fits expectations especially well, audit priority should go up, not down. Counterintuitive, but the logic holds: good results get deployed, bad results merely get ignored — being wrong about the good is costly, being wrong about the bad is cheap.

    4. How the Erratum Happened: Process, Not Inspiration¶

    This bug was not "spotted"; it was forced out by process. The trigger chain:

    1. Independent recomputation requirement: before the L2 line entered formal validation, the pipeline required the return series to be independently recomputed from raw data, not consuming the factory panel's ready-made return column
    2. Cross-reconciliation: the independent recomputation didn't match the factory panel — the differences concentrated near roll days
    3. Root cause localization: comparing the two code paths, the duplicated adjustment step was found
    4. Erratum execution: panel rebuilt, all historical conclusions restated per "corrected numbers", the erratum document numbered and archived (D-9)

    Note step 1 — it is exactly Lecture 4's "component one: independent data recomputation" in the flesh. Had the validation pipeline consumed the factory panel directly, this bug would have been inherited forever, and carry would have marched into shadow monitoring carrying its fake 18% annualized.

    The second-order value of the paper trail also shows here: because old conclusions could be recomputed, the erratum could "restate history" rather than "start over" — the +18% was rewritten as +3.18%, not quietly deleted.

    5. The Chain of Dispositions After the Erratum (More Important than the Erratum Itself)¶

    Finding the bug is only the beginning; an honest system must handle its entire downstream:

    Disposition Content Logic
    Protocol revision Research protocol versioned (v1.0 + D-9 revision), erratum on record Rule changes must leave a trail, preventing "it changed so it never happened"
    Power withdrawal The original OOS decision threshold was based on the +18% expected magnitude; after correction, +3.18% corresponds to extremely low statistical power (350-day window, ~7% under normal approximation) — the original threshold effectively cannot adjudicate Thresholds were designed for large effects; after the effect shrinks the threshold fails, and continuing to use it is formalism
    Priority downgrade L2 demoted from the main line to a low-priority watch line The effect may exist but is too small to justify main-line investment
    Suspend deployment investment Explicit rule: even if later OOS ≥10%, it does not alone trigger deployment Prevents the "eager to redeem after correction" psychology from re-inflating a small effect
    Narrow audit annotations Early audit documents annotated with narrowed applicability (rough diagnostics, excluding rolls); historical numbers unchanged An erratum does not tamper with historical documents; it only delimits their scope of validity

    "Power withdrawal" deserves the most unpacking: a pre-registered threshold (say, "OOS annualized ≥X% with p<0.05") implicitly assumes the effect size is large enough that the test has adequate power at the available sample size. When the effect shrinks from 18% to 3.18%, the same threshold with the same window can barely ever judge a true effect SUPPORTED. Continuing to run a test with no power yields an uninformative result either way (ACCEPT would be luck; REJECT proves nothing). The honest handling is admitting "this question is undecidable at the current data volume" — exactly the Lecture 6 INCONCLUSIVE verdict recurring in another dimension.

    6. Lecture Summary¶

    • Double adjustment = the roll adjustment applied twice; error accumulates systematically in one direction, feeding precisely the carry strategy which holds across rolls
    • Systematic bias makes t-values beautiful; random noise makes them ugly — beautiful numbers are not necessarily cleaner
    • Plausibility bias: the more a result fits expectations, the more easily it passes audit unchallenged; the correction is raising audit priority for expectation-fitting results
    • The four-piece erratum downstream: protocol versioning, power withdrawal, priority downgrade, narrowed historical annotation
    • Independent recomputation is the only institutional means of catching this class of bugs

    Discussion question: Suppose the double adjustment bug affected not carry (long/short legs held across rolls) but a 3-day short-horizon reversal strategy (positions never cross a roll day). Would the contamination be heavier or lighter? What audit principle does that suggest? (Hint: the spatial distribution of the bias determines which strategies ingest how much — rank audits by "the contact surface between the strategy and the defect mechanism", not by the strategy's return ranking.)
