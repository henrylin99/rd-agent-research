    Lecture 5 · Three Candidates All FAIL DSR: Where the Excess Came From, and Where It Went¶

    Chapter 2 · Honest Validation (Lecture 2) Learning objectives: through the complete retrospective of the A-share campaign, understand the concrete mechanics of "leaderboard looks great → independent validation wipes them all out". Prerequisite: Lecture 4.

    1. Case Background¶

    After the A-share campaign (2026-09-18~22) ended, three top candidates were selected from the 290-factor leaderboard via the pre-registered process and sent into the independent validation pipeline. The verdict:

    All three candidates FAIL DSR.

    This lecture's task is to unpack the words "all FAIL": where each candidate's excess appeared to come from, where it actually came from, and under which blade it died at validation.

    2. The First Blade: DSR — You Pay Back as Many Tries as You Made¶

    Recall Lecture 4: DSR corrects the Sharpe for the number of trials N. The three candidates came from the top of a 290-factor leaderboard, so N is not 3 but 290 (strictly it should also count each factor's multiple evaluations — Lecture 2 showed a single factor, momentum_20d, had 23 evaluations; total evaluations across the pool number in the thousands).

    A back-of-envelope: even if a candidate's raw Sharpe were significant in the "single test" sense, after correction for a trial base in the thousands, the required threshold is pushed to a level ordinary numbers can essentially never reach. This is not a harsh threshold; it is simply the fact — the best outcome picked out of a thousand coin flips should not be judged by the standard of a single flip.

    All three candidates fell at this gate. Note this blade's kill logic: it does not say "these three factors are necessarily invalid"; it says "these three, having survived a thousand-way selection, have results insufficient to prove they are not luck". Evidence insufficient — treated as the inverse of the presumption of innocence: not admitted.

    3. The Second Blade: Attribution Decomposition — Is the Excess Alpha or Style Beta¶

    The second thing the validation pipeline did to the candidates: an orthogonalization test (regress on known style factors, look at the residual). The campaign's portfolio-level attribution was even more extreme:

    The equal-weight composite portfolio had OOS IC 0.043, seemingly effective; but after orthogonalizing against three known styles — industry + low-vol + liquidity — 92% of the return was explained away, and the residual alpha's t-stat = 0.14.

    Translated into plain language: the money this "AI factor portfolio" made, 92% of it can be replicated by "buying a pile of low-volatility, high-liquidity stocks with a skewed industry distribution" — all three things known for decades, no need for an AI to compute hundreds of factors overnight. The true residual portion, t=0.14, is statistically zero.

    This is the leaderboard's favorite magic trick: packaging style beta as alpha and selling it to you. The leaderboard doesn't care whether you earned new money — the excess return column does not distinguish "discovering something nobody else knew" from "being heavily loaded on a known style". And for the latter you can just buy a low-vol ETF, without paying the cost of factor mining.

    4. The Third Blade: Turnover and Cost — Gross Return Cannot Buy Back Net Return¶

    Lecture 3 planted the thread: the momentum family with 33 versions is the hardest hit by turnover. The validation pipeline recomputed on a net-of-cost basis: with the top candidates' turnover levels (campaign-measured around 40%+ two-sided per rebalance period) plus A-share trading costs, the gross excess was eroded to insignificance on a net basis.

    The leaderboard shows gross or semi-net numbers (the second of Lecture 2's "three must-ask questions"). The factory has no incentive to show net numbers — it doesn't know your fee schedule, and moreover net numbers would make the leaderboard ugly.

    5. The Deepest Blade: The Benchmark Artifact (Preview of Lecture 8)¶

    During the three candidates' validation, an even more fundamental problem was discovered: the benchmark itself had a convention flaw (under the CSI 300 convention, limit-up stocks were excluded), so a chunk of the "excess" was an artifact created by the benchmark's definition. Recomputed with the honest convention, the biweekly top200 portfolio had no excess at all.

    What makes this discovery stunning is its layer: it is not at the layer of "is the factor good" but at the layer of "is the quantity 'excess' itself trustworthy". However rigorous factor validation is, if the benchmark is wrong, the foundation of every conclusion is wrong. The full treatment is left for Lecture 8 — here you only need to remember the shape of the lesson: validation must dig down to the data foundation, not stop at the factor layer.

    6. Retrospective: The Layered Structure of the Four Blades¶

    Stack the four blades and you see they form a bottom-up audit stack:

    第 4 层  统计充分性   DSR / 多重检验校正     —— 成绩配得上选拔基数吗
第 3 层  收益来源     正交化 / 归因分解      —— 是新钱还是旧 beta
第 2 层  可实现性     换手 × 成本敏感性      —— 净费后还在吗
第 1 层  数据地基     基准口径 / 面板构建    —— 量本身可不可信

    The factory's leaderboard operates only below layer 4, and does only half of it (no correction). The value of independent validation is walking the full stack — the three candidates took one blade at each of the four layers, and none died because "the factor's logic itself was falsified"; all died of insufficient evidence and flawed conventions. This is precisely Lecture 4's sentence in the flesh: validation judges "is it real", not "how good is it".

    7. The Morale Problem: What to Do After All FAIL¶

    After a total wipeout there are two natural emotional reactions, both to be resisted:

    • "Validation is too strict; loosen the thresholds" — loosening thresholds = re-introducing multiple testing. The all-FAIL conclusion was always "insufficient evidence"; loosening the standard does not make evidence sufficient, it only lets false positives in the door.
    • "AI factor mining is useless; abandon it" — an overreaction. The correct reading: the AI factory's value proposition needs restating. Its qualified output is not "factors ready to deploy" but "a stream of candidate hypotheses generated at low cost". Between the candidate stream and a deployable strategy lies an entire validation pipeline — that pipeline always existed for human researchers too (peer review + small live-money trial and error); it was just never formally built as part of the production process.

    The campaign's actual decision was a third path: keep the factory, formally build the validation institution, archive "zero excess under the honest convention" as-is — and then pivot. The first pre-registered experiment after the pivot (Lecture 6) and the only surviving strategy (Chapter 4) were both born after this institution was established.

    8. Lecture Summary¶

    • The three candidates' causes of death, four layers: DSR (thousand-way selection base), style beta posing as alpha (92% explained, residual t=0.14), net-cost erosion, benchmark artifact
    • The leaderboard's magic: doesn't distinguish new money from old beta, shows gross returns, applies no selection correction
    • The correct reading of all-FAIL: the AI factory's unit of output is a "candidate hypothesis stream", not "deployable factors"
    • The four-layer audit stack: statistical sufficiency → return source → realizability → data foundation; miss a layer and killers can slip through

    Discussion question: Suppose a candidate passes DSR and orthogonalization, but its net-of-cost excess is exactly zero (gross excess = costs). Would you judge SUPPORTED, REJECTED, or INCONCLUSIVE? Give your reasoning. (No standard answer — the real campaign's ruling checked items one by one against the threshold table in the pre-registration; the point of this question is to think about "whether your threshold table has this cell".)
